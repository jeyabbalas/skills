**Prompt.** Define distribution and histogram as the book does, and give the one-line Python idiom for computing frequencies with a dictionary.

**Answer.** The distribution of a variable is the values that appear and how many times each appears; a histogram is a mapping from values to frequencies (and the graph of it). Idiom: `hist[x] = hist.get(x, 0) + 1` in a loop (or `collections.Counter`).

**Rubric.** Good requires: both definitions + one working idiom. Easy: names Counter too.

**Prompt.** Apples versus pumpkins: what is the example doing, and when does a mean stop being a good description?

**Answer.** Six half-pound apples are summarized well by their mean; three 1-lb, two 3-lb, and one 591-lb pumpkin have mean 100 lb — misleading because there is no typical pumpkin. A mean describes a sample well only when values cluster around it; with high spread or extreme values there may be no meaningful single-number summary.

**Rubric.** Good requires: the point of the contrast, stated generally (not just retelling the pumpkins).

**Answer.** Pooled variance = (n₁s₁² + n₂s₂²)/(n₁ + n₂); s is its square root. The book's result: the means differ by 0.029 standard deviations — tiny (the male–female height gap is ≈1.7 sd, for scale).

**Prompt.** You have the first-babies results: difference 0.078 weeks (13 hours), d = 0.029. Which summaries would get a story on the evening news, which would reassure an anxious patient — and what does the book say keeps either report honest?

**Answer.** News: the raw difference dramatized (13 hours! or "first babies arrive later"). Patient: the effect size in context (0.029 sd, ~0.2% of a pregnancy) or a probability framing relevant to her. Honesty: persuasion is fine, but reports must acknowledge uncertainty and limitations — the choice of statistic is itself a rhetorical act guided by professional ethics.

**Rubric.** Good requires: audience-appropriate statistic for both cases + the acknowledge-uncertainty principle.
