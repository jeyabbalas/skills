**Prompt.** What is anecdotal evidence, and what are the four reasons the book gives for why it usually fails?

**Answer.** Evidence based on data that is unpublished and usually personal. It fails because of: small number of observations, selection bias, confirmation bias, and inaccuracy (misremembered, misrepresented, repeated inaccurately).

**Rubric.** Good requires: the unpublished/personal definition plus at least three of the four failure modes, named. Easy: all four unprompted. Hard: definition right, failure modes vague.

**Prompt.** Contrast a cross-sectional study with a longitudinal study, and say which the NSFG is.

**Answer.** Cross-sectional captures a snapshot of a group at a point in time; longitudinal observes the same group repeatedly over time. The NSFG is cross-sectional (repeated in cycles — we use Cycle 6, 2002–2003).

**Rubric.** Good requires: both definitions, the contrast, and NSFG placed correctly. Easy: adds the cycle detail unprompted.

**Prompt.** The NSFG is not representative — it is deliberately oversampled. What does that mean, why did the designers do it, and what does it cost?

**Answer.** Three groups (Hispanics, African-Americans, teenagers) were recruited at rates higher than their population share, so each subgroup is large enough for valid inferences. The cost: statistics from the sample no longer transfer directly to the general population (the finalwgt weights exist to fix this).

**Rubric.** Good requires: the mechanism, the why (subgroup power), and the cost. Hard: mechanism only.

**Prompt.** What is a recode in the NSFG, with an example — and what is the book's advice about using them?

**Answer.** A variable calculated from the raw data rather than collected directly — e.g. prglngth is wksgest when available, else mosgest × 4.33. Advice: prefer recodes when available, since they often encode consistency checks, unless you have a compelling reason to process raw data yourself.

**Rubric.** Good requires: definition + the prglngth example (or another real one) + the advice. Hard: definition only.

**Prompt.** In CleanFemPreg, birthwgt_lb values 97, 98, 99 are replaced with np.nan. What do those codes mean, and what concretely goes wrong if you skip this step?

**Answer.** They are special codes: 97 NOT ASCERTAINED, 98 REFUSED, 99 DON'T KNOW. Left in place they behave as real weights and poison computations — the book's example is a bogus 99-pound baby in any mean or histogram. As nan they propagate safely (nan arithmetic yields nan; pandas functions handle nan).

**Rubric.** Good requires: what the codes are + a concrete bogus-result consequence + why nan is safe. Easy: quotes the propagation rule.

**Prompt.** Adding a new column: `df['totalwgt_lb'] = …` versus `df.totalwgt_lb = …`. What does each actually do, and why is the second one wrong?

**Answer.** Dictionary syntax creates a real column in the DataFrame. Dot notation instead attaches a plain Python attribute to the DataFrame object — it is not treated as a column, so later column operations silently miss it.

**Rubric.** Good requires: the attribute-not-column mechanism, not just "use brackets". Hard: knows the rule but not the mechanism.

**Prompt.** Describe the book's validation move for a freshly imported NSFG variable, and how it caught the 51-pound baby.

**Answer.** Compare value_counts() against the codebook's published table per variable. For outcome the counts matched exactly; for birthwgt_lb the binned counts matched but the full listing exposed an impossible value (51 lb), fixed with `df.loc[df.birthwgt_lb > 20, 'birthwgt_lb'] = np.nan`.

**Rubric.** Good requires: the compare-to-codebook move + the loc-based fix. Easy: writes the loc line unprompted.

**Prompt.** The book says to work on two levels at once. What are they, and what does the caseid 10229 example add?

**Answer.** The level of statistics and the level of context. Respondent 10229's outcomes read [4 4 4 4 4 4 1] — statistically unremarkable, but in context the story of six miscarriages before a live birth; the data deserve empathy and the respondents respect.

**Rubric.** Good requires: both levels + what the example shows about reading records as people.
